# Drawing Clothing Layers on the Figure

*A practical guide to dressing human and humanlike figures, from the closest layer to outerwear, for poster and game art.*

## What this guide is for

Clothes should look like garments worn by a body, not colored shapes pasted over an anatomy sketch. To draw them convincingly, first understand the pose and the body underneath. Then decide how each garment fits, where it is attached, how it overlaps the next garment, and how its material responds to gravity and movement.

This guide moves through a common stack: skin and underlayers, underwear or swimwear, pants or skirts, shirts, jackets, and accessories. Not every character wears every layer, and outfits vary by culture, setting, activity, body, and personal choice. Treat the sequence as a drawing method, not a universal dress code.

The same steps work for realistic, comic, pixel-art, fantasy, sci-fi, and humanlike characters. In stylized work, simplify the forms and exaggerate the silhouette, but keep the garment’s construction understandable.

## The order of decisions

For each figure, work in this order:

1. **Pose:** What is the body doing?
2. **Body masses:** Where are the ribcage, pelvis, shoulders, elbows, knees, and other major forms?
3. **Garment plan:** What is worn, in what order, and how does each piece close or attach?
4. **Silhouette:** What shape does the clothing add or hide?
5. **Tension and folds:** Where is fabric pulled, compressed, hanging, or supported?
6. **Construction details:** Seams, waistbands, cuffs, collars, straps, pockets, fasteners.
7. **Material and light:** How do thickness, stiffness, sheen, and value make the layers separate?

Do not begin by drawing wrinkle marks. Decide what is pulling the cloth first. A fold should have a cause.

## 1. Draw the body underneath

Make a complete, simple figure before dressing it. The underdrawing can be a mannequin rather than a finished nude figure: head, ribcage, pelvis, limb cylinders, joints, hands, and feet. Mark the pose’s center of gravity and the direction each major mass faces.

Use a low-opacity construction layer. Keep it visible while drawing the clothes, then hide it to inspect the garment-only read. This avoids common errors such as a sleeve not meeting the wrist, pants cutting through the pelvis, or a jacket ignoring a bent elbow.

Mark the following landmarks on the body:

- base of neck and shoulder points;
- chest and ribcage bottom;
- waist and pelvis top;
- front and side of the hips;
- crotch and seat direction;
- elbows, wrists, knees, and ankles;
- major joints that the garment bends around.

These points help place garment openings and fold sources. They are not all visible in the final drawing.

### Pose before outfit

Clothing changes shape with the pose. A jacket on a straight-standing figure differs from the same jacket on a figure reaching overhead. Pick the action first, then dress that action.

If the pose is hard to read, solve it in a silhouette with no clothing detail. Only then add garments. The clothing should clarify the action, not hide a weak pose.

## 2. Decide how each garment fits

Before drawing an item, choose a fit category:

- **Close fit:** follows the body closely; few large folds, but still creases at bends and seams.
- **Tailored fit:** follows the body with designed ease; shape is controlled by darts, panels, shoulder structure, or a waistband.
- **Relaxed fit:** hangs slightly away from the body; larger folds and more visible air space.
- **Oversized fit:** outer contour is significantly larger than the body; sleeves, torso, and hems have their own weight and volume.
- **Rigid or armored:** holds a designed shape; bends at joints or between plates instead of wrinkling like cloth.

A garment’s fit controls its silhouette and fold density. Do not use the same wrinkle treatment on leggings, a loose shirt, and a leather jacket.

### Leave room for thickness

Every layer has thickness. A heavy coat sits outside the shirt; the shirt sits outside the body. Even in simplified drawings, offset the outer contour where a collar, waistband, hem, padded shoulder, or cuff adds bulk. If all layers share one contour, the outfit looks painted onto the figure.

## 3. First layer: underwear, swimwear, and close-fitting garments

This layer sits closest to the body and establishes how later garments meet it. Depending on the character and scene, it may be underwear, a swimsuit, a sports bra, shorts, a bodysuit, a leotard, a tank, or a base layer. Keep the design appropriate to the character, setting, and intended rating.

### Draw the garment as constructed clothing

1. Trace the body’s large forms lightly: chest, ribcage, waist, pelvis, and upper thighs.
2. Decide the garment’s coverage and design before drawing its edge. Think about how it is cut, supported, fastened, and made.
3. Draw the main fabric panels as shapes that wrap around the body, not lines that simply outline anatomy.
4. Place straps, bands, edges, or seams where they would support or join the garment.
5. Add small tension marks where straps pull or fabric stretches over a bend.
6. Add only a few folds. A close-fitting stretchy garment generally has fewer, shallower folds than loose fabric, but bends at the waist, hip, shoulder, elbow, and knee can still crease.

For a bikini or swimsuit, show how the top and bottom are separate pieces or connected by a design. Establish bands, straps, seams, and the direction the fabric wraps. Avoid outlining every anatomical contour with equal emphasis; let the garment’s construction and value describe the fit.

For a bodysuit or leotard, treat it as a continuous shell with seams and openings. Show where the fabric stretches over the torso and gathers at joints. If it has a zipper, panel, or emblem, place that detail along a believable seam or front plane.

### Do not confuse tight with wrinkle-free

Stretch fabric can still pull diagonally from a strap, seam, or waistband. It can bunch at a compressed joint and form shallow ripples near an edge. Use subtle marks; a dense web of creases will make the garment look loose or damaged.

## 4. Bottom layer: pants, shorts, skirts, and waistbands

Pants wrap around the pelvis and continue as two separate legs. This construction matters more than decorative wrinkles.

### Place pants step by step

1. Draw the waistband as a band wrapping around the waist. Its top edge follows the pelvis’s tilt; it will not be perfectly horizontal in a tilted pose.
2. Mark the center front, side seams, and crotch connection. Think of the pelvis as a volume inside the clothing.
3. Draw the seat and front rise as fabric connecting the two legs. Do not make the crotch a sharp V unless the garment is deliberately stylized.
4. Draw each pant leg as a separate tube around the thigh and lower leg. Taper or widen based on the cut.
5. Check whether the knees are bent. Add compression folds on the inside of the bend and tension lines on the stretched side.
6. Let the fabric gather at the ankles, boots, or cuffs only if the cut and material allow it.
7. Add fly, pockets, belt loops, seams, and hems after the main fit works.

The two pant legs should not mirror each other automatically. A bent knee raises or compresses its fabric differently from a straight support leg. Show that difference.

### Jeans, trousers, shorts

- **Jeans:** heavier, structured fabric; broad folds at hip and knee, firmer creases, visible seam lines, pockets and waistband construction.
- **Dress trousers:** cleaner outer line, controlled creases, fewer random folds, shaping around the seat and knee.
- **Work pants/cargo pants:** fuller fabric with pockets that project from the leg; pocket flaps must follow the leg’s turn.
- **Shorts:** keep the waistband and crotch construction; show the hem as a band or folded edge, with fabric volume above it.
- **Leggings:** close to the form but not painted on; use seam placement and restrained stretch creases to describe the garment.

### Skirts and hanging panels

Treat a skirt as fabric attached at a waistband or yoke. First mark the attachment line and the direction gravity pulls. Then decide whether the fabric is narrow, gathered, pleated, stiff, or flowing. Folds radiate from the waist or other support points, but do not draw identical spokes all around; vary their width and direction according to movement and cloth volume.

If the figure twists, the waistline can tilt while the hem lags or swings in another direction. That difference can show motion. Check that the skirt’s volume has a plausible attachment and does not look like a flat triangle pasted behind the legs.

## 5. Tops: shirts, tanks, sweaters, and uniforms

A top wraps around the ribcage and shoulders, then hangs or stretches over the waist. Its neckline, armholes, sleeve seams, and hem explain how it is built.

### Draw a shirt on a posed body

1. Draw the neck opening around the base of the neck. The opening turns with the chest.
2. Place the shoulder seam from neck/shoulder area toward the arm. On a dropped-shoulder shirt, move it down the upper arm.
3. Draw the torso fabric around the ribcage and waist. A loose shirt hangs away from the body; a fitted shirt follows more of its form.
4. Attach sleeves around the armhole. Keep the sleeve volume distinct from the upper arm beneath it.
5. Set the hem over, into, or above the pants waistband. Show the overlap: shirt over waistband, tucked behind waistband, or cropped above it.
6. Add folds from the pose’s pull points and compression areas.
7. Add collar, placket, buttons, pocket, print, or insignia only after the fit reads.

A simple T-shirt often has a few folds radiating from the armpit, shoulder, or waist, plus compression at a bent elbow or tucked hem. A loose shirt has larger, slower folds. A tank top exposes more of the shoulder and armhole, so the edge construction needs to be clear.

### Sweaters and hoodies

Knit fabric is usually thicker and softer than a thin shirt. The hem and cuffs often have ribbed bands that compress the material. A hoodie adds a hood with volume, drawstrings, a front pocket, and thicker seam edges. Draw the hood as a three-dimensional form behind or around the neck; do not place it as a flat shape on the back. If it is down, show the opening and fabric stack.

### Uniforms and workwear

Uniforms often use structured panels, badges, stitching, reinforced knees, or a specific collar. Keep details aligned to the body’s perspective. A patch on a turned sleeve bends around the arm; a chest badge follows the torso plane. Use a small number of identifying design cues rather than covering every surface in insignia.

## 6. Jackets and outer layers

A jacket sits over the shirt and often has more structure and thickness. It may be open, zipped, buttoned, belted, padded, or rigid. Draw the jacket as a new outer volume; do not merely trace the shirt and add lapels.

### Jacket construction sequence

1. Draw the jacket’s outer silhouette, allowing extra volume over the shoulders, chest, arms, and back.
2. Decide how it closes: zipper, buttons, snaps, belt, or open front. Establish the front edges and overlap.
3. Draw the collar or lapels as folded planes connected to the neckline.
4. Add shoulders and sleeves. A structured shoulder may project; a soft shoulder may droop.
5. Decide where the jacket separates from the body. Open panels can reveal the shirt underneath and create a strong layered edge.
6. Add compression where the arms bend or the torso twists. Add stretched folds on the opposite side.
7. Draw cuffs and hem as bands with thickness. Let the inner layer peek out only where the opening makes sense.
8. Add pockets, seams, lining, patches, studs, or wear after the form is clear.

### Open jacket versus closed jacket

An open jacket creates two front panels. Those panels hang from the shoulders and may swing with motion. Show their thickness at the lapels, zipper edge, and hem. If the jacket is open, the shirt underneath should continue behind it. Avoid drawing the shirt’s outer contour through the jacket.

A closed jacket has a central fastening line. Keep that line on the front plane and let it bend with the torso. In a twist, the two halves may not look symmetrical. Buttons or zipper teeth should follow the closure line rather than float independently.

### Different outerwear reads differently

- **Denim jacket:** seams, yoke, cuffs, collar, pockets; medium stiffness and broken creases.
- **Leather jacket:** broad shape, sharper highlights, few hard creases, strong lapel/zipper design.
- **Bomber:** rounded torso volume, ribbed cuffs and waistband, gathered sleeve fabric.
- **Blazer:** structured shoulder, lapels, controlled waist, limited folds.
- **Long coat:** hangs from shoulders, with broad vertical panels; movement is clearest at hem and opening.
- **Padded coat:** larger, puffed sections separated by seams; show the loft without outlining every stitch.
- **Armor or hard-shell gear:** plates overlap; show gaps, straps, and joints. Do not wrinkle rigid plates like fabric.

## 7. Accessories and attachment logic

Every accessory should appear attached or supported. Belts wrap around the waist and pass through loops. Straps cross over the shoulder and disappear behind the body or connect to a bag or instrument. Holsters, suspenders, armor straps, necklaces, scarves, and capes should have visible anchor points.

Use these checks:

- What holds this item up?
- Where does it touch the body or garment?
- Does it pass in front or behind the torso?
- Does it bend, hang, or stay rigid?
- Does the pose move it, or does it lag behind?

Draw attachment points first, then the accessory. A guitar strap, for instance, should cross the shoulder and connect to the instrument; it should compress or disappear beneath a jacket edge where appropriate. A belt should follow the waist angle and wrap around the side of the body.

## 8. Folds: draw the forces, not random wrinkles

Most clothing folds can be explained by a few forces:

- **Tension:** fabric pulled between two points, such as shoulder to wrist or waistband to knee.
- **Compression:** fabric pushed together at an elbow, waist, knee, or ankle.
- **Gravity:** fabric hangs from a collar, shoulder, belt, waistband, or hem.
- **Support:** a seam, belt, cuff, or body surface holds the fabric up.
- **Movement:** cloth trails, swings, or lags behind the body.

### Practical fold pass

1. Mark the anchor or tension points: shoulder, elbow, wrist, waist, hip, knee, cuff, or hand gripping cloth.
2. Draw arrows for the pull direction.
3. Identify compression zones on the inside of bends and beneath belts or straps.
4. Add the largest fold shapes first. These should explain the garment’s volume and movement.
5. Add smaller creases only near important seams or bends.
6. Shade the fold as a form: one plane catches light, the adjacent plane turns into shadow.
7. Remove lines that do not show tension, compression, construction, or material.

Fold patterns vary. A hanging sleeve can form long folds; a bent elbow creates a fan or zigzag of compression; cloth stretched between shoulder and wrist can make diagonal pulls; soft fabric at the waist can bunch into overlapping arcs. Study the garment and pose rather than memorizing one universal wrinkle pattern.

### Material changes the folds

- **Thin/stretch fabric:** follows the body closely; shallow ripples and sharper pulls at tension points.
- **Cotton T-shirt:** soft folds around armpits, waist, and elbows; moderate shape retention.
- **Denim/canvas:** heavier folds with firmer creases and less flutter.
- **Leather/vinyl:** broad reflective planes and fewer, sharper creases.
- **Wool/knit:** soft, rounded folds and thicker hems or cuffs.
- **Silk/light fabric:** long, fluid folds that react readily to movement.
- **Armor/plastic:** hard panels and mechanical joints; no cloth-like wrinkles on the rigid sections.

## 9. Show which layer is in front

Layer order needs to be visible at overlaps. Decide the order for every crossing: shirt under belt, jacket over shirt, hand over cuff, strap over coat, coat over bag, and so on.

At an overlap, use one or more of these cues:

- a clean contour break where the front garment covers the rear one;
- a visible edge thickness, hem, cuff, or seam;
- a small cast shadow from the upper layer onto the lower layer;
- a value or color shift that separates the pieces;
- a clear continuation of the rear garment on either side of the overlap.

Avoid tangent lines where two edges merely touch. Either separate the contours with a small gap or make one garment decisively pass over the other. Check cuffs, waistbands, collars, jacket fronts, crossed arms, belts, and straps especially carefully.

### Draw hems and edges with thickness

A hem is not just a line. It turns around the garment and may show stitching, a folded edge, ribbing, or a shadow beneath it. At small scale, one extra dark or light band can make the edge read as cloth thickness. Use the detail only where it helps distinguish the layers.

## 10. Use light and shadow to separate layers

Choose a consistent light direction. Then decide which garment planes face the light and which sit in shadow. Give overlapping layers their own shadow boundary: a jacket can cast a shadow onto a shirt; a cuff can shadow the wrist; a belt can darken the fabric beneath it.

For graphic or pixel-inspired art, group shadows into clear dark shapes instead of outlining everything. Near-black can separate jacket from shirt, arm from torso, and pant leg from coat. Keep the lightest accents on a few edges, seams, or folds. If every seam and fold is equally bright or dark, the outfit becomes noisy.

At poster scale, use value and silhouette before texture. The figure should read in grayscale and as a small thumbnail before you add fabric grain, stitching, scratches, or printed patterns.

## 11. Drawing clothes for a poster

Posters are viewed at different distances. The outfit needs one or two strong identifying shapes that survive reduction: a wide lapel, bright uniform stripe, shoulder pads, long coat, striped shirt, hood, bandana, or distinctive silhouette.

Try this order:

1. Fill the figure and outfit as one dark silhouette.
2. Separate the major garment layers with two or three value groups.
3. Check the face, hands, and key prop against the clothing shapes.
4. Keep folds and seams near focal areas more specific; simplify less important areas.
5. Add color and texture after the black-and-white read works.

Avoid drawing a full fashion plate if the poster only needs a quick read. A few deliberate details carry more character than uniform microtexture everywhere.

## 12. Blender workflow for layered clothing studies

Blender can help check pose, camera, overlap, and garment volume before the final 2D pass. It does not replace drawing decisions.

### Pose and body block-in

1. Use a simple mannequin made from ellipsoids, boxes, or tapered cylinders.
2. Set an orthographic camera for the poster view.
3. Pose the body and check the silhouette from the actual camera angle.
4. Save a render or viewport screenshot as a reference layer.
5. Draw the underlayer on top of the pose. Keep the body reference at low opacity.

### Build garment layers as separate drawing layers

In Grease Pencil, 2D drawing software, or a compositing setup, separate:

- body construction;
- closest garment layer;
- pants/skirt;
- shirt/top;
- jacket/outerwear;
- accessories;
- shadows and highlights.

Name layers by garment and overlap. For example: `BODY_GUIDE`, `BASE_LAYER`, `PANTS`, `SHIRT`, `JACKET_FRONT`, `JACKET_BACK`, `STRAP`, `SHADOWS`. This lets the artist correct a hidden seam or move an overlap without redrawing the figure.

For a more procedural setup, make reusable garment components as hand-authored templates: collars, cuffs, waistbands, pockets, lapels, straps, sleeve types, and hems. Use parameters for scale, rotation, fold direction, or material. Do not assemble these by random placement. Their attachment points and overlap order need to be designed and reviewed.

### Check in grayscale and at final size

Render a flat color pass, shadow pass, and highlight pass if that helps iteration. Inspect the result at the intended poster size. If a seam or fold vanishes, decide whether to enlarge it, simplify it, or remove it. Do not leave thin lines that flicker or turn to noise after scaling.

## 13. Exercises

### Exercise A: same body, three fits

Draw one standing pose. Dress it in a fitted shirt, a relaxed shirt, and an oversized hoodie. Keep the body underneath identical. Compare the outer silhouette, sleeve volume, hem position, and fold size. This teaches fit more directly than drawing three unrelated characters.

### Exercise B: layer order

Draw a torso and one arm. Make three variations:

1. Shirt tucked into pants, belt over shirt and waistband.
2. Shirt outside pants, jacket open over it.
3. Shirt under a closed jacket, strap crossing on top.

Use only contour overlap and one shadow shape to show the order. Add details after the overlaps read.

### Exercise C: one jacket, three poses

Draw the same jacket on a neutral stance, a reaching pose, and a seated pose. Keep the jacket design consistent. Change the folds according to shoulder pull, elbow bend, waist compression, and gravity. If the folds stay the same in all three drawings, they are decorative rather than pose-driven.

### Exercise D: pixel-value clothing

At small resolution, render a figure with underlayer, pants, and jacket using only black, dark gray, light gray, and white. Keep each garment’s shape distinct. Add color only after the layers read in grayscale.

## 14. Troubleshooting

| What looks wrong | Check | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Clothes look painted on | Is there any gap, thickness, or outer volume? | Offset loose garments from the body; add visible hems and overlaps |
| Outfit hides the pose | Does the silhouette merge limbs into torso? | Change negative spaces, sleeve shape, crop, or arm position |
| Wrinkles look random | Can each fold be traced to a pull, bend, support, or hanging point? | Remove unexplained marks; redraw the large folds from force directions |
| Every garment has the same folds | Did fit and material change? | Use different fold scale and edge stiffness for each fabric |
| Jacket looks like a shirt with lapels | Does it have outer volume and independent sleeve shape? | Widen the silhouette and show thickness at collar, cuffs, hem, and front edges |
| Pants look like tubes | Is the pelvis and crotch construction clear? | Draw seat, rise, waistband, and two separate leg volumes |
| Layers are hard to distinguish | Are overlaps and value groups clear? | Break contours, show edges, add cast shadows, or adjust values |
| Accessories float | Where are the anchor points? | Show the belt, strap, clasp, pocket, or hand that supports the item |
| Detail turns to visual noise | Are seams and folds equally sharp everywhere? | Keep detail at focal areas and simplify the rest |
| The garment fails in a new pose | Was it copied from a neutral template unchanged? | Redraw pull directions, compression, and hanging volume for the new pose |

## 15. Final review checklist

Before calling a clothed figure finished, confirm:

- The body pose reads before costume detail.
- Each garment has a clear fit and a believable outer silhouette.
- The layer order is obvious at collar, cuff, waist, and accessory overlaps.
- Folds come from tension, compression, gravity, support, or movement.
- Materials have different weights and surface behavior.
- Seams and closures follow the form in perspective.
- The hands, face, and important prop remain visible.
- The outfit reads in silhouette and grayscale at poster size.
- Details are concentrated where they help identify character, setting, or story.
