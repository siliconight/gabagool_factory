# Drawing Humans and Humanlike Figures for Poster Art

*A practical guide for artists building expressive figures for concert, band, film, collectible, and game-world posters with procedural tools.*

## Purpose

A figure on a poster has a different job from a character in a close-up illustration. It must read quickly, support the headline, fit the mood, and remain clear when the poster is viewed across a room or compressed onto a small in-game texture. The figure can be realistic, comic-book bold, pixelated, masked, monstrous, robotic, or only partly human. The same fundamentals still apply: a clear pose, believable connected forms, a readable silhouette, and deliberate light and dark shapes.

This guide teaches a process an artist can repeat by hand, then explains how to turn that process into a controlled Blender workflow. “Procedural” should mean that tools help explore, organize, and vary deliberate choices. It should not mean that anatomy, gesture, or composition are left to chance.

## The finished figure should answer four questions

Before drawing, write one short answer for each:

1. **Who or what is this?** A singer, boxer, monster, masked hero, card champion, dancer, alien, mascot, or unknown silhouette?
2. **What are they doing?** Singing into a mic, bracing for impact, turning toward a light, casting a spell, escaping, posing, or collapsing?
3. **What should the viewer feel?** Excitement, danger, comedy, glamour, mystery, sadness, power, or unease?
4. **What must read first?** The face, raised hand, weapon, instrument, costume emblem, or overall pose?

If these answers are vague, detail will not rescue the figure. Make the action and focal point clear first.

## 1. Start with reference and a pose brief

Use references to understand weight, joints, clothing, and lighting. Use several references when a pose combines elements that no one image provides. Take your own photos when possible, ask a collaborator to pose, or use a poseable 3D model to check a difficult view. Do not trace one reference into a finished design unless you have the rights and that is the intended workflow. Reference is evidence about form; the drawing is your designed interpretation.

Write a compact pose brief before sketching. Example:

> “A tired club singer leans into the microphone, shoulders rounded, one hand gripping the stand. The head tips toward the light. The coat and cable sweep down-left. The pose should feel intimate, not heroic.”

For a more graphic poster:

> “A masked champion plants one foot forward and turns the chest toward the viewer. One arm guards the face; the other reaches out of frame. The silhouette must read as confident and dangerous.”

The brief keeps the drawing from becoming a generic standing figure with costume added afterward.

### Build a reference sheet

Collect 3–6 references that answer different questions:

- One reference for the action or balance.
- One for the camera angle or foreshortening.
- One for clothing folds or material.
- One for face, hair, or distinctive accessories.
- Optional: one for lighting and shadow shapes.

Reduce each reference to a few notes. For example: “weight on back leg; pelvis tilts; near hand foreshortened; hard light from upper left.” Avoid copying every surface detail.

## 2. Gesture: find the action before the anatomy

Gesture is the pose’s direction and energy. It is not a decorative curve drawn down the middle of every body. Look for the strongest directional path through the pose: head, chest, pelvis, supporting foot, or the extended hand. Describe it with a C-curve, an S-curve, or a straight/angled thrust. Use whichever best describes this pose.

### A 60-second gesture pass

1. Draw a line or two for the main action. Keep them light and quick.
2. Mark the head as a simple oval with a centerline showing its turn.
3. Add the ribcage and pelvis as two separate tilted masses.
4. Connect their centers with a flexible spine line. Do not force it into a smooth S if the pose is straight or angular.
5. Add the shoulder line and hip line. Make their tilts relate to the action.
6. Mark hands and feet as simple directional wedges or circles.
7. Draw the arms and legs as single flowing strokes from joint to joint.

At this stage, you should be able to identify what the figure is doing without facial features, fingers, or clothing detail. If not, simplify the pose further.

### Give the pose rhythm

A lively pose often has curves and angles that relate without mirroring each other. Think of the eye travelling through a sequence: one large sweep through the torso, a counter-tilt at the pelvis, then a smaller echo in an arm or leg. Repeated curves can harmonize. Curves offset from each other create a sense of rhythm. A sharp directional break can add emphasis, but too many abrupt breaks make the pose noisy.

Try this check:

- Is the head tilted or turned differently from the ribcage?
- Does the ribcage oppose or echo the pelvis?
- Do the two arms do different jobs?
- Do the legs show different bends or different amounts of weight?
- Is there one deliberate angle that makes the pose snap?

Avoid drawing paired limbs as matching brackets. In a natural pose, one arm may compress while the other stretches; one leg may carry the weight while the other relaxes.

### Weight and balance

Ask where the figure’s weight is supported. If the figure stands, imagine a vertical plumb line from the center of the torso or pelvis toward the floor. It should land over a supporting foot or between the feet. In a jump, fall, or lunge, the line can miss the base because the body is moving; then show the direction and force of that movement clearly.

For a standing pose, make one leg the support and the other the gesture leg. Tilt the pelvis over the support leg, then angle the ribcage to counterbalance. This small asymmetry often makes a neutral pose feel inhabited.

## 3. Build the body from large forms

Do not outline a flat “person shape” and then decorate it. Build a few masses that turn in space. A useful starter mannequin has:

- **Head:** an egg or box-like mass with a face centerline.
- **Ribcage:** an egg, barrel, or tapered box. It has a top, bottom, front, and side.
- **Pelvis:** a bucket, bowl, or short box that can tilt independently.
- **Limbs:** tapered cylinders or paired tapered forms with a joint at each bend.
- **Hands and feet:** simplified wedges before fingers and toes.

Draw through forms lightly. Add a centerline around the ribcage and pelvis to show which way each points. If the torso turns, the ribcage and pelvis should not both face the viewer by default.

### Step-by-step construction pass

1. Draw the gesture skeleton: head, shoulder point, elbow, wrist, hip, knee, ankle.
2. Connect points with directional lines. Check the action and balance.
3. Wrap the ribcage and pelvis around the centerline as separate solids.
4. Add neck, shoulder masses, and the connection from ribcage to pelvis.
5. Build upper arms, forearms, thighs, and lower legs as tapered volumes.
6. Use overlapping forms at joints. The upper arm enters the shoulder; the forearm overlaps or turns around the elbow; the thigh enters the pelvis.
7. Add hands and feet as wedges showing their direction and plane.
8. Draw the outer contour only after the forms make sense.

Use simple anatomy landmarks to orient the forms: base of the neck, shoulder tip, bottom of ribcage, navel area, front points of the pelvis, elbow, wrist, kneecap, ankle bones. These are guides, not dots to connect into a formula.

### Proportions are adjustable scaffolding

A common adult proportion guide measures the figure in head lengths. It is a starting point, not a rule every character must obey. A realistic adult may be around seven to eight heads tall depending on style and body type. A short, comic figure may be five or six heads tall. A towering fantasy figure may be nine heads or more. Decide the intended design and keep the proportions consistent within it.

Compare relationships instead of relying only on a memorized number:

- Where does the elbow fall relative to the waist?
- How far does the hand reach down the thigh?
- How long is the forearm compared with the upper arm?
- How does the head compare to the shoulder width?
- How much of the leg is thigh versus lower leg?

For a stylized figure, exaggerate with a plan. A giant head can increase expression. Long legs can make a character elegant or uncanny. Oversized hands can make a gesture readable. Exaggeration feels intentional when the main proportions agree with each other.

## 4. Draw limbs as connected volumes

Arms and legs are not parallel pipes. They taper, turn, overlap, and change direction at joints.

### Arms

Block the shoulder as a mass attached to the ribcage. The upper arm runs from shoulder to elbow; the forearm runs from elbow to wrist. Mark the elbow and wrist before shaping the contour. Check the arm’s action as a whole, then check the bend. The elbow is not always visible on the outside edge; it moves with the turn of the arm.

For a foreshortened arm, do not make the limb longer just because you cannot see its full length. Show the near end larger, overlap the forms, and use a clear directional axis from shoulder through elbow to wrist.

### Legs

Attach the thigh into the pelvis, rather than beginning it as a separate stick. Mark hip, knee, and ankle. The thigh and lower leg usually taper in different directions, and the knee creates a change of plane. In a weight-bearing leg, make the foot feel planted and align the leg’s major masses toward it. In a relaxed or moving leg, let the foot angle follow the motion.

### Hands and feet

At poster scale, a clear hand gesture matters more than five individually perfect fingers. Start with a palm block and a thumb wedge. Group fingers into one or two readable shapes, then separate only the fingers that carry meaning: pointing, gripping, waving, strumming, or casting. Feet can begin as a heel-to-toe wedge. Show the shoe or foot angle clearly before adding laces or seams.

## 5. Make the silhouette carry the pose

Fill the figure with black, reduce it to thumbnail size, and look away for a moment. Can you still tell what the figure is doing? Can you separate the arms from the torso? Are the legs distinct? Is the head angle visible? Does the prop read?

If two shapes merge, change the pose before adding internal lines. Common fixes:

- Move the elbow away from the waist.
- Separate the legs with a wedge of negative space.
- Tilt the head or shoulder to expose a gap.
- Rotate the hand so its gesture reads against the background.
- Shift the prop to a cleaner angle.
- Push one form forward and another back through overlap.

The empty spaces between body parts are useful shapes. Look at the triangle between an arm and torso, the gap between the legs, or the opening beneath a bent arm. Adjust these spaces for clarity.

### Use overlap to show depth

Choose which form is in front at each connection. A clear overlap at shoulder, elbow, wrist, hip, knee, or hand can tell the viewer how the body turns. Avoid tangent edges where two contours touch and continue together without a clear front/back relationship. Either separate the shapes or overlap them decisively.

## 6. Add the head, face, and expression

Treat the head as a volume, not a flat mask. Start with a cranium shape, then attach the jaw. Add a vertical centerline to show the turn and a horizontal brow/eye line to show tilt. The eyes, nose, and mouth wrap around the front plane; they do not stay flat and frontal when the head turns.

At small poster scale, simplify features into the few marks that carry the expression:

- Brow direction and eye shadows.
- Nose bridge or nostril shape.
- Mouth opening or smile line.
- Hairline, facial hair, glasses, mask, or distinctive headwear.

Do a small grayscale thumbnail. If the expression disappears, increase the size or contrast of the feature that matters, or let the face become a stronger silhouette. Keep high detail for the focal face; simplify faces in secondary figures.

For nonhuman faces, use the same construction logic. Establish a cranium, front plane, centerline, and jaw or muzzle. Then change the proportions: longer muzzle, wide-set eyes, horns, mandibles, beak, mask, or mechanical plates. The viewer still needs to understand where the face points and what it is feeling.

## 7. Dress the figure with form and story

Clothing should describe the body and the material. First draw the body underneath; then decide where the fabric stretches, hangs, bunches, or breaks away from it. Folds usually gather around compression points—elbows, armpits, waist, knees—and stretch across opposing areas. Do not scatter wrinkle lines evenly over the whole outfit.

Use a small set of clues to distinguish materials:

- **Leather or vinyl:** broad, hard highlights and a few sharp creases.
- **Denim or canvas:** firm folds, seams, patches, and worn edges.
- **Silk or loose fabric:** long flowing folds with softer changes.
- **Metal or armor:** clear plane changes, bright edge hits, dark separations.
- **Fur or feathers:** grouped tufts or directional clumps, not a line on every strand.

Choose costume details that support the character and poster story: instrument strap, team badge, torn sleeve, wrist tape, uniform piping, medallion, cape, tool belt, stage jewelry, or a distinctive pattern. Give each detail a reason to be there. At small scale, a few large motifs work better than many tiny buttons.

## 8. Light the figure with a small value plan

Before rendering color, use three to five value groups:

1. **Silhouette/darkest:** outline, shadow side, hair, costume separations.
2. **Shadow:** underside of forms and cast shadows.
3. **Base/midtone:** the broad body and clothing planes.
4. **Light:** planes facing the main light.
5. **Accent/highlight:** a few bright edges or reflected-light marks.

Pick one main light direction and keep it consistent across head, torso, limbs, and props. Use a rim light only where it helps separate the figure from the background. If every edge glows, the figure loses its focal point.

For pixel-inspired art, cluster shadow pixels into deliberate shapes. Use black and near-black to separate overlapping parts; use white or the lightest value for small focal hits. Avoid outlining every part with the same thickness. Thicker dark accents can gather in creases and contact points, while lit edges can disappear. That variation makes the form feel less mechanical.

## 9. Compose the figure for a poster

A poster figure is part of a layout. Reserve space for the headline, event information, and margins before finalizing the pose.

### Thumbnail test

Make 3–6 tiny poster sketches. In each, test a different figure placement:

- Full figure centered with a strong diagonal.
- Head and shoulders cropped large, leaving a title band.
- Figure pushed to one side, with a prop or gesture pointing into the text.
- Two figures facing or opposing each other.
- A silhouette behind large type.

Use black shapes and blank areas first. Do not start with faces or costume texture. Ask whether the figure and headline compete or work together.

### Crop with intention

Cropping at the top of the head, wrists, knees, or ankles can look accidental. Either include the joint clearly or crop through a deliberate section of the form. Let a hand, instrument, wing, or weapon break the frame only when it reinforces the design. Keep critical gestures away from trim and texture edges.

### Poster families

- **Concert / band poster:** use diagonal movement, a strong performer silhouette, instruments or mic shapes, dramatic stage light, and energetic clothing. Keep the face or instrument as a clear focal point.
- **Movie poster:** use a readable central action or iconic silhouette. Separate foreground, middle ground, and background figures by scale, value, and overlap. Avoid filling every gap with floating heads unless that is a deliberate genre convention.
- **Collectible / fantasy poster:** design a distinctive hero, creature, or emblem silhouette. Use a clear power pose, controlled ornament, and a small number of magical or graphic effects that lead back to the focal point.
- **World-building advertisement:** let the figure sell the place or event—bartender, mechanic, bouncer, performer, local champion, mascot. Clothing, pose, and props should reveal the setting.

## 10. Procedural workflow in Blender

Blender is useful for pose blocking, camera checks, shape generation, texture passes, and repeatable layout variations. Keep the artist’s decisions visible and editable.

### A. Block a pose with a mannequin

1. Set the camera to orthographic for a flat poster view.
2. Add simple low-detail meshes for head, ribcage, pelvis, upper/lower arms, and upper/lower legs.
3. Use ellipsoids or tapered cylinders; rotate and scale them to match the gesture.
4. Place simple joint markers at shoulder, elbow, wrist, hip, knee, and ankle.
5. Move the camera until the silhouette and foreshortening read.
6. Save the rough render as a reference layer or draw-over guide.

A 3D mannequin is a measuring aid. Do not accept its default anatomy, stiff fingers, or generic stance as finished artwork. Redraw the contours, exaggerate gesture, and simplify shapes to fit the chosen style.

### B. Draw over with Grease Pencil or curves

Use Grease Pencil for expressive contour, hatch, and hand-drawn marks. Use curves or polygon shapes for cleaner poster silhouettes and vector-like cutouts. Keep separate collections/layers for:

- gesture and construction;
- body silhouette;
- interior shadow shapes;
- costume and props;
- highlights and texture.

Lock the reference/mannequin layer while drawing. Name layers by purpose so variations remain understandable.

### C. Make controlled variations

Create a small set of intentional options rather than random anatomy:

- choose one of 3 gesture paths (C, S, angular);
- choose one of 3 head turns and 2–3 head tilts;
- choose a support-leg side, then counter-tilt pelvis and ribcage;
- choose one arm action (grip, point, guard, reach, strum);
- choose from a designed set of costume silhouettes;
- vary pose proportion within a range that artists have reviewed.

Use named presets and sliders for scale, rotation, lean, arm spread, leg bend, and head angle. Preview every generated combination as a black silhouette. Reject combinations with merged limbs, impossible balance, hidden focal gesture, or awkward tangencies. Procedural systems should generate candidates, not approve the final art.

### D. Render for a 2D poster asset

Use a flat orthographic camera, transparent film if exporting a cutout, and a limited palette/material setup. Render separate passes or layers for silhouette, shadow, base color, highlights, and effects if compositing flexibility matters. Export a PNG at the intended poster dimensions or larger, then resize down with the project’s chosen filtering approach. Inspect for one-pixel gaps, edge halos, and accidental transparency.

## 11. Pixel-art and low-resolution adaptation

If the poster is deliberately pixelated, design the figure at the final pixel resolution or at a clean integer multiple. Do not draw at a huge size and shrink without checking the resulting clusters.

1. Set a small canvas and define the pixel grid.
2. Block the pose as two or three flat value masses.
3. Check the silhouette at native size.
4. Place shadow clusters where forms overlap or turn away from the light.
5. Add only the interior marks that distinguish the pose, face, costume, and prop.
6. Reduce the palette and remove isolated single pixels unless they serve as deliberate highlights.
7. View on the actual in-game wall from normal play distance.

Use clusters that follow form: a curved shoulder shadow, a wedge beneath the chin, a dark gap between arm and torso, a grouped highlight on the cheek or instrument. Avoid evenly spaced noise. In a grayscale pass, confirm that the focal head, hands, and prop separate clearly before adding color.

## 12. A full practice exercise: singer with a microphone

This exercise can be done on paper, in a 2D app, or with Blender as a pose reference.

### Pass 1: action (2 minutes)

Write: “The singer leans into the mic, gripping the stand, coat trailing behind.” Draw 6 tiny gestures. Keep the head, ribcage, pelvis, hands, and feet only.

### Pass 2: choose and construct (10 minutes)

Pick the clearest thumbnail. Mark shoulder, elbow, wrist, hip, knee, ankle. Build ribcage and pelvis as separate masses. Check which foot bears weight. Draw the mic stand as a strong straight line that contrasts with the body’s curves.

### Pass 3: silhouette (5 minutes)

Fill the figure black. Separate gripping hand from face and stand. Make the coat silhouette echo the movement without hiding the torso. Adjust the arm if the hand merges into the microphone.

### Pass 4: face, clothing, and prop (15 minutes)

Turn the head toward the mic. Add only a few face marks. Draw the jacket folds where the arm bends and where the coat pulls from the shoulder. Give the mic a clear shape and add one cable curve that leads the eye toward the title or event details.

### Pass 5: values and palette (10 minutes)

Choose a main stage light. Add a dark plane beneath the chin and along the far side of the body. Use one bright hit on the face, hand, or mic. Limit colors to a small, coordinated set.

### Pass 6: poster test (5 minutes)

Place the art inside a poster thumbnail with headline and date. View it small and in grayscale. If the title is unreadable or the figure becomes a blob, simplify and rearrange before adding texture.

## 13. Common problems and direct fixes

| Problem | Likely cause | Try this |
|---|---|---|
| Figure looks stiff | Pose built from mirrored sides or straight mannequin limbs | Draw gesture first; make the two sides do different jobs; tilt ribcage and pelvis differently |
| Figure looks like rubber | Gesture curves are not anchored to clear forms | Rebuild ribcage and pelvis as solids; mark joints and show overlaps |
| Limbs look disconnected | Arms or legs attach as lines instead of volumes | Draw the shoulder and hip masses; overlap the upper limb into the torso |
| Pose does not read at small size | Too much interior detail, weak silhouette | Fill figure black and redesign negative spaces |
| Hands look like mittens | Palm direction or thumb placement unclear | Draw a palm wedge and thumb first; separate only the meaningful fingers |
| Character feels generic | Costume detail added without a character idea | Write an action and personality brief; add 2–3 story-specific shapes or props |
| Anatomy feels overworked | Too many muscles and contour lines | Remove marks that do not explain gesture, form, or identity |
| Figure floats | Support point and gravity are unclear | Align the torso over the supporting foot or show a clear leap/fall direction |
| Shading turns to noise | Every area receives equal texture | Group shadow masses; reserve detail and sharp contrast for the focal area |
| Procedural poses look repetitive | Same template and symmetry repeated | Vary action, weight, camera, crop, and negative spaces; review each silhouette |

## 14. How to practice and critique

Do short studies from varied references. A useful 20-minute session:

- 5 poses at 30 seconds: gesture only.
- 3 poses at 2 minutes: gesture, ribcage, pelvis, limb direction.
- 1 pose at 5 minutes: add volumes and overlap.
- 1 poster thumbnail at 5 minutes: place that figure with a title block.

Critique in this order:

1. Can I tell what the figure is doing?
2. Does the pose feel balanced or intentionally in motion?
3. Do the body masses turn in space?
4. Does the silhouette separate the important parts?
5. Does the head/hand/prop focal point read at the target size?
6. Does the figure fit the poster’s genre and layout?
7. Which three marks can be removed without losing clarity?

Keep a small pose library of approved gesture sketches, mannequin screenshots, and silhouette studies. Label each by action and view: “low-angle guard,” “leaning singer, side view,” “two-character faceoff.” Reuse them as starting points, not as fixed templates. Change the action, weight, camera, and silhouette so repeated figures do not look stamped from one mold.
