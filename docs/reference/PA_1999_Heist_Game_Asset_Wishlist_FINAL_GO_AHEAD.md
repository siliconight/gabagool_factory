## 1. The direction

**Pennsylvania, 1999. A new stereo in an old car. A beautiful sign over a business with terrible hours. Somebody has spent real money on the sound system and refuses to replace the carpet.**

People are working, showing off, making music, getting dressed, repairing things, hosting parties, and finding ways to get paid. Some businesses are doing well. Some are coasting on a reputation. Some have money moving through rooms the customers never see.

The player should want to spend time in this world before the shooting starts. They should notice a jacket they would wear, a car they would drive, a record sleeve they would pick up, a booth they would claim, or an office they immediately distrust.

The tone is observant, skeptical, and composed. People know the difference between a good tool and an expensive one. They can recognize somebody putting on a front. They have loyalties and pleasures that survive disappointment. There is room for danger, vanity, affection, dry humor, and the occasional excellent bad decision.

**The production goal: make objects that reveal what people do, what they want, and where they choose to spend money. Give those objects a visual presence worth remembering.**

This pass keeps the original 224 families and their IDs. It replaces broad mood labels with specific build directions, upgrades the music and street culture layer, removes the named ethnic faction templates, and gives the locations stronger visual anchors. The original gameplay coverage remains: structures, services, people, equipment, animation, audio, effects, and interface.

Develop original characters, business identities, graphics, music, and silhouettes from these qualities. A strong reference should help explain a design decision; it should not become a checklist of recognizable franchise props.

**Production rule:** describe materials, shape, function, mood, and ownership directly. Do not request a recognizable character, costume, vehicle, level, interface, soundtrack, logo, or promotional image from another property with its name changed. Multiple references and cosmetic alterations are not automatic clearance. See Section 13 for the asset-specific checks.

### A working taste test

An asset should earn attention through one or more of these:

- **Form:** an excellent silhouette, proportion, material pairing, or piece of lettering.
- **Use:** the object makes a task or space immediately understandable.
- **Desire:** someone clearly wanted this, saved for it, customized it, or takes pride in it.
- **History:** a specific decision or change remains visible.
- **Play:** it creates a useful landmark, route, threshold, objective, or social encounter.

Background objects can be quiet. A plain wall can make a great lightbox look better. A clean, ordinary chair does not need a tragic backstory.

## 3. Rules that develop the taste

### 3.1 Put the visual budget where somebody put their money

For each room or vehicle, decide what its owner values. Then spend the detail budget there.

| Owner or user | Where the money went | What they tolerated | Assets that tell the story |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Young car-audio installer | Speakers, wheels, a good jacket. | Plain apartment furniture, an old work bench. | Carefully wired display wall, clean alloy wheel, worn sofa, receipts for parts. |
| Longtime bar operator | Refrigeration, reliable taps, keeping regulars comfortable. | An unfashionable interior. | Excellent maintained bar hardware, repaired booth, old but legible sign. |
| Club promoter | Sound, a striking flyer, one impressive entrance. | A rough service corridor and cheap office furniture. | Speaker stack, silver-on-black poster, curtain, folding desk. |
| Established business owner | Privacy, a good suit, quality furniture in the room where deals happen. | Dated equipment elsewhere. | Proper wool coat, heavy table, ordinary fax machine, discreet door lettering. |
| Record-store employee | Music, sleeves, personal curation. | Improvised shelving and a secondhand chair. | Staff selections, handmade dividers, listening headphones, carefully stored records. |
| Bank or developer | Confidence and a sense of permanence. | Cold staff spaces behind the presentation. | Stone threshold, polished handrail, aspirational brochure, utilitarian breakroom. |

These are individual examples. Change the priorities for another owner. Avoid giving every working person a broken chair and every wealthy person good taste.

### 3.2 Let different generations coexist

Build layers that people could have added over time: an older storefront, a later renovation, a recent purchase, today's activity. Choose only the layers supported by the location.

An ornate former hall can hold a cheap suspended ceiling and a serious new sound system. An office can keep a handsome wooden desk because the owner likes it. A younger tenant can own very little and keep every object immaculate. A new store can simply be new.

The year appears through the newest layer. Use a small number of well-chosen contemporary purchases and graphics rather than filling every surface with era reminders.

### 3.3 Give Y2K design a buyer and a purpose

| Design language | Build it as | Where it belongs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brushed silver and black glass | Stereo faceplates, rack units, a display pedestal, a compact player's controls. | Car-audio shop, electronics counter, a proud owner's room. |
| Translucent plastic | Small electronics shells, storage cases, select retail display pieces. | Newer consumer goods, a youth bedroom, a carefully chosen office purchase. Verify the specific design's date. |
| Chrome lettering and precise geometry | A music sleeve, dance-night flyer, store promotion, event wristband. | Club, record shop, ticket counter, promoter's office. Use a genuine layout hierarchy. |
| Speed, movement, technical confidence | Cropped photography, diagram-like marks, condensed type, restrained motion graphics. | Tuner or sound business, music advertising, equipment packaging. Every mark needs a compositional job. |
| The approaching millennium | One event poster, a booking sheet, fresh promotional packaging, a computer-service notice. | Businesses planning a party or selling an upgrade. Use exact dates only after choosing the mission date. |

**Date gate:** all in-world hardware, clothes, logos, slang, print processes, and screen designs need a plausible pre-2000 reference. A broadly Y2K-looking object can still be too late. Later games inform presentation; they do not grant permission for later products inside the setting.

### 3.4 Place the Gothic character in the city itself

Keep the church roof beyond the loading dock, a stone institutional entrance, a dark stair with patterned glass, iron bars against a bright shop window, a long coat crossing a doorway. Use age, verticality, shadow, and ceremony.

Give the bank, church, funeral business, civic building, and old club their own architectural and graphic histories. A pointed window is one possible detail. An enormous municipal doorway or an empty banquet room can carry just as much weight.

### 3.5 Treat street culture as people making things

Make a skate deck with an original graphic, a repaired amplifier, a mix CD with fictional track names and its owner's handwriting, a record sleeve with good typography, a tagged wall with a consistent fictional writer's hand, a carefully altered car, and a flyer made by somebody with actual layout instincts.

Use separate graphic authors. The record shop, bank, local band, church fundraiser, and discount electronics wholesaler should have different tools, budgets, handwriting, and judgment. Recurring graffiti belongs to a small authored set of fictional writers; placement reflects reach, visibility, surface, and local repetition. Keep enough empty wall for the marks to matter.

Community backgrounds shape particular people, homes, meals, languages, photographs, and relationships. Develop criminal networks through who owes whom, who owns what, and who can move money. The first version's named Italian/Irish/Russian crew packages are retired. Establish characters and businesses before naming an organization.

### 3.6 Write with restraint

Most names should sound usable in a phone call. Use proprietor names, services, streets, geographic reach, family history, or a modest commercial boast when they fit. Draft a business card and invoice alongside the main sign: the name must work there too.

All business, band, product, event, and game names remain provisional until checked for relevant existing uses and confusing similarity. An invented name can still collide with an existing one. Develop a distinct overall identity as well as distinct wording; keep the dated reference and the permission to reproduce it as separate questions.

Most signs sell something or give an instruction. Most people speak to the person in front of them. Humor can come from a very expensive chair in a tiny office, a formal sign beside an improvised repair, or an employee's small correction to an officious memo. Let the player notice it.

Avoid making every brand a sex joke, every criminal a philosopher, every flyer a slogan, or every poor room a wreck. Save overt exaggeration for a business or character with a reason to perform.

### 3.7 Make a few bold choices

This world should have energy, sex appeal, menace, and fun. Believability gives the art something to push against. Permit strong departures in selected views while keeping the underlying object and place understandable.

| Bold choice | Concrete build direction | What holds it together |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A coat with presence | A long, sharply cut dark wool coat with a broad collar and an unmistakable moving hem. | It belongs to one character; the rest of the wardrobe and movement support the choice. |
| A club room that feels enormous | Tall curtains, a low central stage, oversized speaker stacks, and a long clear view through the room. | Venue architecture and actual equipment placement justify the proportions. |
| A genuinely good ugly interior | Dark floral carpet, smoked mirror, brass rails, and oxblood seating composed with discipline. | Commit to one owner's complete taste rather than collecting unrelated kitsch. |
| A flash of new money | A spotless silver stereo installation, a polished car, or a too-large desk inside an otherwise ordinary business. | One purchase reveals an appetite or ambition. |
| A hard graphic interruption | Acid-yellow paper with black oversized type on a mostly subdued street; a cobalt sleeve with a severe silver image. | Give the designer a strong composition and limit competing accents nearby. |
| A dramatic criminal threshold | A heavy private door at the end of a bright, ordinary staff corridor; a still figure framed behind patterned glass. | Staging, space, and character behavior create tension before effects are added. |

Adult venues can have sincere glamour, awkward economics, attractive design, and working staff. Cheapness, sensuality, and good taste can coexist. Give the room a committed visual idea before adding any jokes.

### 3.8 Let music leave physical evidence

Select distinct music interests for people and venues. Suggested directions below are creative choices for original compositions and artwork; they are not a prescribed soundtrack or claims about the reference games.

| Scene | Musical direction to explore | Physical assets it produces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Skate / independent shop circle | Punk, hardcore, independent rap, or another deliberately chosen local scene. | Demo tapes, photocopied show bills, small-label sleeves, worn deck graphics, a practice-space amplifier. |
| Dance promoter / late-night room | Breakbeat, house, techno, or drum-and-bass selected for a particular promoter. | Turntable and mixer setup, record cases, silver print, ticket drawer, speaker stacks, equipment hire paperwork. |
| Car-audio customer | Bass-forward rap or dance music chosen by that individual. | Carefully selected equipment, a labeled mix, speaker fitment, display cards, and a car that reflects the purchase. |
| Private listening / safehouse | Downtempo, soul, rock, rap, or older records with a personal reason to remain in rotation. | A small curated collection, handwritten sequencing, a favored chair, and a good system assembled over time. |

Choose among these for each person. Allow disagreements between people sharing a room. A record collection should feel selected, and an original track should sound as though someone wanted to make it.

## 4. Palette, materials, and shape

| Location | Base materials and colors | Deliberate accent | Main visual move |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Corner store / deli | Cream laminate, aluminum, warm brick, dull tile. | Red fascia, blue-white coolers, colorful stock. | A bright, legible shop nested inside an older block. |
| Record / sound shop | Charcoal, silver equipment, pale laminate, plywood edges. | Cobalt, acid yellow, or one translucent color. | Tight display composition around products worth wanting. |
| Bar / social room | Dark stained wood, oxblood vinyl, bottle glass, brass. | Warm shaded lamps or one saturated sign. | Low, intimate pools of light with a clear view to the service threshold. |
| Nightclub / converted hall | Black-painted repairs, tall old openings, exposed service hardware. | Deep blue, warm red, or silver graphics selected for that venue. | One decisive entrance and a strong stage or sound-system silhouette. |
| Garage / depot | Concrete, painted steel, dull aluminum, paper labels. | Fleet color, a clean tool chest, a single excellent car. | Machinery and working clearances make the composition. |
| Bank / civic interior | Stone, bronze, cream paint, dark timber. | Quiet institutional blue or green. | Mass, symmetry, scale, and the transition into plain staff space. |
| Home / safehouse | Owner-specific furniture, textiles, wallpaper, practical lamps. | One person's collection, jacket, plant, or stereo. | Taste varies between people and even between corners of a room. |

Use broad readable shapes with enough bevel, thickness, material separation, and texture control to feel authored. Spend geometry on silhouettes, hands-on parts, faces, vehicle profiles, and important furniture edges. Keep large areas calm enough for local detail to register.

Make daylight scenes as convincing as night scenes. A sign, a car, and a garment should survive neutral light before dramatic lighting is used to present them. Wet pavement, green casts, haze, and bloom are scene choices with costs; none is a universal style preset.

## 5. How to use the wishlist

| Priority | Meaning | Production rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **P0** | Foundation for a complete first heist. | Build functional placeholders, then finish the families actually used by that mission. |
| **P1** | Core release expansion and stronger identity. | Add when the reusable loop works and a location needs the family. |
| **P2** | Specialist destination or additional variety. | Scope the mission before committing to the kit. |

The first mission remains a neighborhood convenience store with a cash office, upstairs tenancy, rear delivery yard, and escape van. Each row is a family, not one mesh. Required corners, backs, moving parts, collision, states, sound, and interaction points belong to the deliverable.

**Read the proposed object and its use together.** Select the variants appropriate to the owner and mission. A family is a source of related choices; do not place all its examples in every room. Mechanic-dependent entries remain conditional on approved gameplay.

| Club event | Booking board and invitation. | Hall arrangement and later clearing state. | Time passes and rooms have changing uses. |

A recurring graphic should retain its identity across a sign, sleeve, box, shirt, or scrap. Repetition is useful when it communicates distribution or loyalty. Keep chronological dependencies explicit so the same unique object does not appear in two places simultaneously by accident.

### Room dressing order

1. **Purpose and owner:** choose the work, occupants, spending priorities, date, and current activity.
2. **Fixed construction:** complete the shell, openings, services, and major equipment.
3. **Working arrangement:** place furniture, customer/staff routes, storage, and cleaning access.
4. **Composition:** select a main view, one anchor, supporting shapes, and quiet areas.
5. **Personal choices:** add a small number of belongings or purchases tied to specific people.
6. **Current task:** add a coherent delivery, meal, repair, sale, or event state.
7. **Surface history:** apply only the wear, repairs, fading, and marks justified by use.
8. **Play review:** preserve AI paths, door swings, objective access, rescue space, combat readability, and four-player movement.

Furniture needs a plausible delivery path. Every object needs support or suspension. Wires, pipes, ducts, and drainage need destinations. Personal decoration should be visible from natural play views; the mission should not require inspecting tiny text to understand its basic situation.

## 11. Acceptance: does this have the intended taste?

Review in a composed scene with the intended camera. Do not approve only a turntable render.

- [ ] The asset has a clear function or a strong compositional reason to exist.
- [ ] Its shape, material, and important graphic survive normal play distance.
- [ ] A person or institution could plausibly choose it, use it, and maintain it.
- [ ] Any conspicuous purchase, customization, repair, or neglect follows that owner's priorities.
- [ ] The design has a plausible 1999 reference basis; later cultural influences remain in presentation rather than unverified products.
- [ ] Graphic hierarchy works before distress, fading, or texture is added.
- [ ] Different businesses and subcultures appear to have different authors.
- [ ] Incorporated third-party materials have documented permissions for the intended use; names and complete designs have received the applicable similarity review.
- [ ] It has enough ordinary surrounding space to register.
- [ ] Humor and personal evidence reward noticing without explaining the whole character.
- [ ] Required states, collision, animation, sound, sockets, and reset behavior are complete.
- [ ] It has passed neutral-light and actual mission-light review in Godot.
- [ ] The full four-player encounter remains readable and within measured performance targets.

For a location, ask five plain questions:

1. **What would I remember about this room tomorrow?**
2. **What does somebody here take pride in?**
3. **What were people doing five minutes before we arrived?**
4. **What connects this place to another part of the city?**
5. **Would the room still have character in daylight with nobody explaining it?**

If the answers depend entirely on dirt, colored lights, nostalgic logos, or witty signs, revisit the objects and composition. If every answer is elaborate, simplify. The world needs room for routine.

## 13. Originality and rights pass

**Scope:** review of the written brief, not a search of every possible existing design or a review of finished meshes, textures, audio, brands, contracts, or shipped software. No obvious instruction to reproduce a specific existing asset was identified. The main risks were open-ended franchise references and insufficiently specific sourcing requirements; those directions have been revised. All 224 families remain.

Copyright protects original expression rather than underlying ideas. Names may raise trademark issues even when copyright does not protect the words themselves. These distinctions support keeping broad period and genre qualities while independently developing the actual work. [R4, R5]

### Default production choices

| Asset area | Required approach for this project |
| --- | --- |
| Packaging, storefronts, sports, bands, fictional organizations | Create the name, emblem, typography, layout, and overall presentation together. Review relevant existing uses. A near-spelling or a logo swap is not our originality method. |
| Cars, electronics, weapons, costumes | Research function and period construction; author the final design. Inspect the complete result for distinctive borrowed combinations. Route intentional replicas to a specific rights review. |
| Posters, maps, photos, murals, screens, paintings | Use original work or material with documented reuse rights. Redrawing, distressing, tracing, or embedding an image in a texture does not by itself establish permission. |
| Music, samples, TV, voices, effects | Keep composition and recording rights separate in the record. Use original or appropriately licensed material, including any samples and loops. A new recording does not automatically clear someone else's song. [R6] |
| Models, textures, fonts, scans, stock packs | Retain the actual license or grant and check commercial game use, modification, distribution/embedding, and attribution as applicable. Downloadability and a seller's description alone are insufficient evidence. |
| Real places and architecture | Study local construction and geography. Separately review any exact building reproduction, distinctive business identity, artwork, or reference photograph incorporated into production. |
| AI-assisted outputs | Record the inputs and source assets, and review the resulting image, model, text, or sound. An output labeled “original” or “fictional” is not evidence that it avoids recognizable copying. |

These are project production defaults, not a claim that every third-party reference or depiction is unlawful. Any deliberate reliance on licensing, public-domain status, fair use, parody, or another legal basis needs its own recorded assessment.

### Before approving a finished asset

1. Record who made it and which third-party material, if any, is incorporated.
2. Verify the relevant license or written grant and any required attribution; distinguish research access from reproduction rights.
3. Compare the complete design against its references and any recognizable resemblance noticed during review. There is no reliable “change a percentage” rule.
4. Check proposed game and fictional brand names for relevant existing uses; this written pass has not performed trademark clearance.
5. Resolve unclear permissions or deliberate close reproductions before release, with qualified counsel where needed. Preserve the decision with the asset.

**Direction to the asset creator or level builder:**

> Build a functioning Pennsylvania place in 1999. Decide who uses it, what they value, and what they have spent money on. Give the important objects strong shapes, good materials, and graphics made with actual taste. Let work, music, clothes, cars, private habits, and public presentation leave different kinds of evidence. Make a few things worth wanting. Leave room for ordinary life. Prove the result at player height, in motion, with the heist running.
