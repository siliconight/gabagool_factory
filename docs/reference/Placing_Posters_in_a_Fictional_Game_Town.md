# Placing Posters in a Fictional Game Town

*A guide to making posters feel put up by people, fit their buildings, and still read clearly as game art.*

## The goal

A town’s posters should look like they have a reason to be there. Somebody paid to advertise a show, taped up a missing-person notice, pinned a flyer beside the register, or covered an old bill with a newer one. The placement tells the player who uses the building, what the neighborhood cares about, and how much time has passed.

The scene still needs art direction. “Human” does not mean every poster lands at a random angle or every wall becomes a collage. Use a few clear compositions, believable attachment points, varied but controlled heights, and different layers of age. Keep important information readable from the player’s normal viewing distance.

## The main rule: every poster needs a reason

Before placing a poster, answer:

- **Who put it there?** A venue, business owner, promoter, landlord, neighbor, city worker, or passerby?
- **Who should see it?** People approaching the door, waiting in line, using the counter, walking through an alley, or riding a bus?
- **Why this surface?** Is it visible, permitted, easy to reach, protected from rain, or already a known place for flyers?
- **How long has it been there?** Fresh, curling at the corners, partly covered, sun-faded, or reduced to scraps?

A poster that cannot answer these questions may still work as decoration, but it should be treated as deliberate background texture rather than evidence of town life.

## 1. Think in placement zones, not blank walls

Divide each building into surfaces with different jobs. A storefront might have a front window, door glass, brick pier, side wall, vestibule, interior counter area, and back alley. Each has a different audience and permission level.

| Zone | Typical use | Placement character |
|---|---|---|
| Front window | Events, specials, services, public notices | Visible from sidewalk; often kept reasonably neat by the business |
| Door or entry glass | Hours, rules, one urgent flyer, small event notice | Limited space; attached around handles and existing decals |
| Exterior wall beside door | Local shows, neighborhood events, services | Hand-height or eye-height; layered selectively |
| Interior vestibule | House rules, event schedule, notices for customers | More curated; protected from weather |
| Counter or register | Small local ads, takeout menus, service cards | Dense but orderly; placed where customers wait |
| Hallway or stair landing | Notices for residents, building services, gigs | Clusters near turns, mailboxes, doors, or light sources |
| Side wall facing foot traffic | Large event poster or repeated campaign | One strong placement may be more believable than a full wall of flyers |
| Alley service door | Staff notices, band flyers, handbills, older pasted bills | Lower maintenance, more overlap and wear; still needs plausible access |
| Utility box or pole | Small handbills and stickers | Narrow vertical surfaces; repeat only when the spot is accessible and locally used |
| Interior bathroom | Small flyers, graffiti, band stickers | Tight clusters around mirrors, stalls, or sink area; rarely a full-size clean poster |

Do not fill every available plane. Empty wall is part of the composition. A single flyer near a door can say more than twenty posters arranged evenly across the facade.

## 2. Match placement to the building

A poster should reflect what happens in the space and who controls it. Build a small placement profile for each building type.

### Bar, dive bar, or club

- Put the main show or event poster near the entrance, window, or host stand.
- Use smaller flyers near the bar, bathroom entrance, coat area, or stage corridor.
- Let the inside carry more layers than the street-facing facade.
- Give the business one or two maintained surfaces: a framed event board, a clear menu area, or a wall behind the bar.
- Put older, damaged flyers in peripheral spots. Keep the current event poster easiest to notice.

### Convenience store or corner shop

- Keep the front window and door useful: hours, lottery, food specials, service notices, and a small number of community ads.
- Cluster local flyers near the register or community board, where customers have time to read.
- Use a few small stickers or handbills on side surfaces. Avoid turning the whole window into one solid wall of paper if the shop needs visibility into and out of the store.

### Laundromat

- Use a noticeboard, change machine area, folding tables, and entrance glass.
- Include practical material: machine rules, lost items, services, local events, room rentals.
- Make the main board more organized than the alley-facing wall; people maintain information they rely on.

### Music venue or record shop

- Use a dedicated flyer wall or case near the entrance, box office, record bins, or stairs.
- Show a mix of upcoming events, old bills, and local acts. Let the current show stand out through position, freshness, or scale.
- Use music-related visual language but keep the overall wall pattern varied across locations.

### Apartment lobby or rooming house

- Place notices near mailboxes, entry buzzers, stairwells, or laundry rooms.
- Favor practical notices and resident-made messages. A polished tour poster can appear, but should feel like it was posted for a reason.
- Avoid inaccessible placement above shoulder height if residents are expected to read it.

### Alley, loading dock, or back entrance

- Use paste-ups, torn event bills, crew notices, and older layers where people can reach them.
- Let one side of a door or wall become a known posting surface, while adjacent surfaces remain mostly bare.
- Add weathering, grime, and torn edges in ways that match rain runoff, sun exposure, foot traffic, and cleaning.

### Office, bank, or public-facing service

- Use official boards, window notices, lobby stands, or taped paper near a service area.
- Keep unofficial music or neighborhood flyers limited to places where staff or visitors could plausibly put them.
- Contrast formal notices and informal flyers through paper, alignment, typography, and attachment method.

## 3. Vary the installation, but keep it believable

People do not place every poster with the same care. A promoter may carefully align a current event bill. Someone else may tape a handbill quickly to a pole. A store manager may use a frame or corkboard. The variation should come from different people and circumstances, not noise added uniformly.

Use a deliberate mix of:

- square or nearly level placement;
- slight rotation from hurried taping;
- uneven margins or one corner lifting;
- tape at two corners, one strip across the top, pushpins, staples, or paste;
- a poster tucked behind glass or under a clear holder;
- one edge hidden by a doorframe, pipe, or adjacent poster;
- one or two overlaps where the newer sheet covers part of an older one.

Keep most posters readable as physical objects. If every sheet is tilted, curled, torn, and overlapped, the scene feels designed to look messy. Reserve the strongest irregularities for a few focal clusters.

### Height and reach

Place most readable flyers where a standing person can put them up and another can read them without strain. Vary height by context: a business window may be arranged at eye level; a board may have a fixed layout; a pole may have stacked handbills; a low wall may collect older sheets. Avoid a repeated perfect centerline across all buildings.

Posters placed far above reach need a reason: a ladder, a pasting pole, an old sign location, or placement by someone working from a window. If there is no reason, lower it or use a preexisting sign frame.

### Edges and corners

A fresh poster usually adheres near its edges. To show it coming loose, peel a corner or edge, not a random hole in the center. A curled corner should cast a small shadow and expose the old layer or wall beneath. Tears should follow handling, weather, or the shape of the sheet. Avoid identical corner curls copied across the whole town.

## 4. Give poster clusters a history

A believable cluster has sequence. It records what happened before the current moment.

A useful three-stage stack:

1. **Old base layer:** a faded bill, damaged notice, old paint, or bare wall.
2. **Middle layer:** a partly torn or partly covered flyer that shows previous activity.
3. **Current layer:** the newest event, service, or public notice, attached more cleanly and easiest to read.

Not every location needs all three. Some businesses scrape walls clean. Some neighborhoods reuse the same posting spot. Some alleys accumulate layers for months. Let the maintenance level differ by owner and location.

### Aging should follow exposure

Think about what the surface experiences:

- Direct sun fades color and can bleach the top or exposed side.
- Rain runs down from ledges and gutters, leaving streaks and lifting lower edges.
- A sheltered vestibule keeps paper cleaner.
- Hand contact darkens edges near handles or counters.
- Cleaning removes some paper, sometimes leaving scraps or adhesive.
- New paint may cover old layers, with pasted corners or ghosted rectangles remaining.

Use this selectively. A procedural dirt mask applied equally to every poster makes them all feel the same age. Age belongs to the poster’s location and history.

## 5. Design for game readability

Posters are both world detail and visual communication. Players may notice a bold design from across a street but need to approach before reading small text. Decide which parts need to read at which distance.

- **Across the street:** large color block, headline, central image, venue mark.
- **At the doorway:** event name, date, price, simple service message.
- **Close inspection:** small print, fictional sponsors, stains, tape, fine illustration, torn edges.

Keep the focal poster’s silhouette and headline clear. If the player needs to find a door, objective, or interactable, do not bury that cue in high-contrast poster clutter. Use quieter values near key navigation points, or deliberately reserve a clean margin around important gameplay objects.

Vary the size of paper for a reason: full-size event poster, half-sheet flyer, small tear-off notice, business card, menu, or sticker. Repeating one identical rectangle at every scale is a strong procedural tell.

## 6. Build poster walls as compositions

Treat a cluster as a designed shape inside the environment. Sketch it as a thumbnail before adding details.

Try these arrangements:

- **One hero poster and two supporting pieces:** the current show or announcement is the focal point.
- **Loose vertical stack:** flyers were added over time to a pole or narrow wall.
- **Board with mixed sizes:** pinned notices share a maintained community surface.
- **Wide pasted patch:** several overlapping bills create a graphic rectangle on an alley wall.
- **Sparse window:** one or two notices preserve visibility through the glass.
- **Old cluster with a clean gap:** papers were removed, leaving residue around a more recently cleared area.

Use asymmetry, but maintain a visual center or flow. Let one poster be larger, newer, brighter, or more intact. Supporting flyers can be smaller, more muted, or partly hidden. Avoid equal spacing, equal rotation, equal contrast, and equal damage across a group.

## 7. Procedural placement: author the rules, vary within them

A procedural system should decide among approved possibilities. It should not choose arbitrary coordinates on every wall. Define a posting zone and its rules for each wall or building type.

### Placement data per surface

For each candidate surface, record:

- surface type and orientation;
- usable bounds and blocked areas;
- allowed poster sizes and aspect ratios;
- minimum and maximum height;
- maximum poster count or coverage;
- approved attachment methods;
- weather/exposure category;
- maintenance level;
- whether layering is allowed;
- whether the surface is gameplay-critical;
- a location tag such as `front_window`, `bar_flyer_wall`, or `alley_pasteup`.

A wall surface may be a manually authored rectangle or a marked region on a mesh. Use it as a container for composition, not as a license to cover every square inch.

### A controlled generation recipe

1. Choose the building’s poster profile: maintained, casual, layered, neglected, or nearly bare.
2. Select a cluster layout designed for that profile.
3. Pick posters that fit the location and intended audience.
4. Assign one focal item and a small number of supporting items.
5. Place the focal item within an approved height and margin range.
6. Add bounded variation to rotation, spacing, and offset. Use small changes, not unrestricted randomness.
7. Apply age and damage from the surface exposure and poster history.
8. Check that doors, windows, signs, objective markers, and sightlines remain readable.
9. Review the result from player height, at normal play distance, and close up.

Keep the random seed stable per building or level so artists and designers can reproduce a result. Store overrides for hand-tuned hero locations.

### Useful authored variation controls

| Control | Suggested options |
|---|---|
| Layout | single, stack, board, overlap patch, window pair |
| Density | empty, sparse, moderate, dense focal cluster |
| Angle | level, slight clockwise, slight counterclockwise |
| Attachment | tape, staple, pushpin, paste, frame, glass holder |
| Age | fresh, handled, faded, rain-worn, partly removed |
| Coverage | isolated sheet, partial overlap, layered stack |
| Care | business-maintained, casual, neglected |

These are art-direction controls. Choose option ranges per location; do not make every option available everywhere.

### Avoid common procedural tells

- Same poster asset repeated nearby or across neighboring buildings.
- Identical spacing, height, rotation, or mounting pattern on every wall.
- Damage maps reused without regard to exposure.
- Flyers pasted over doors, handles, vents, windows, signs, or electrical boxes.
- A dense poster wall with no obvious reason for being there.
- Perfectly complete, crisp posters in a location described as neglected.
- So much overlap that the player cannot identify the current or focal sheet.
- Random angles large enough that paper appears to defy gravity.

Use a local repetition check: within the player’s current view, vary the poster art, size, age, and cluster shape. Reuse across town is fine when it reads as the same campaign; change its condition or placement as it travels through the world.

## 8. Make the 3D placement sit on the building

In Blender, test poster size and height with a human-scale proxy, door, window, or known wall measurement. Confirm the sheet sits against the surface and faces the expected direction. A tiny offset from the wall prevents z-fighting; too much offset makes it float. Use a thin plane or a shallow mesh for a physical sheet, especially when the edge needs to cast a subtle shadow or curl. Use a flat material or texture for a clean pasted image.

For Godot 4.7, choose the representation based on the surface and art need:

- A thin mesh or quad works well for a flat poster with a clear physical outline and straightforward UVs.
- A decal projects onto a surface and can help with pasted bills or wall graphics, but check renderer support and project settings for the target renderer.
- Use a shared atlas or texture set where practical, and keep poster materials consistent with the project’s filtering and mipmap choices.
- For pixel-art posters, test texture filtering and mipmaps in the actual scene; nearest filtering may preserve pixel clusters, while other settings may be better for a deliberately softened print.
- Avoid transparent materials unless the poster actually needs transparency. Paper itself is usually opaque; a clean cutout edge can use alpha scissor if needed.

Do not use a billboard for a paper poster attached to a wall; it should remain oriented with the building. For angled or curved surfaces, align the poster to the local surface or use a suitable mesh/decal method.

Check the poster under the final scene lighting. Paper should respond to light and wall shading. An emissive poster that remains equally bright at night may read like a screen unless that is intentional.

## 9. A hands-on placement pass for one building

Use a corner bar as an example:

1. **Walk the exterior.** Mark the front window, door, brick pier, side wall, and alley door as separate zones.
2. **Choose the business’s behavior.** The bar keeps its window mostly clear but allows a few local show flyers near the door.
3. **Pick a focal poster.** Place the current band bill at eye height on the brick pier, where it is visible from the sidewalk.
4. **Add evidence of time.** Put one older, faded bill partly beneath it. Leave a small exposed strip of older paper at the edge.
5. **Use another zone for another purpose.** Put a handwritten staff notice inside the service entrance, not on the front facade.
6. **Vary attachment.** Tape the old bill; use paste or two staples for the new one.
7. **Protect function.** Keep the window view, handle, business hours, and entrance path clear.
8. **Check from play space.** Walk past at normal speed. The bar identity and current show should register before fine print.
9. **Take a screenshot.** Review for tangents, repetition, unnatural height, floating sheets, and over-dense decoration.

Now repeat the method for a laundromat or apartment lobby with a different density, layout, and maintenance profile. The town should have shared habits, but each building should feel managed by a different person.

## 10. Quick review checklist

- Does every cluster have a believable audience and reason to be there?
- Does the poster sit within human reach or have a visible reason for being higher?
- Does the building type explain what kinds of notices appear?
- Are window and door functions preserved?
- Is one item clearly current or visually important?
- Do age, damage, and cleanliness match the surface’s exposure and maintenance?
- Are size, rotation, overlap, and attachment varied in controlled ways?
- Does the cluster read as a composition from game distance?
- Are objective markers and navigation cues still easy to see?
- Can the result be reproduced and art-directed with a stable seed or override?
