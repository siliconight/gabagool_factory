# Poster Atlas Art Direction Feedback

## Make the set feel more hand-made, specific, and dynamic

This feedback is for the submitted club, bar, alley, and store poster sheet. The current work has a clear retro pixel-art language. Keep the limited palettes, blocky type, and readable sign-like structure. The next pass should make each poster feel more individually designed instead of generated from the same layout.

## What is working

- The posters read as a coordinated set of fictional in-world graphics.
- The pixel lettering and limited palettes support the retro game look.
- The club, bar, alley, and store groups already have different color moods.
- The copy gives the locations local character and humor.

## Main opportunity

Several pieces repeat the same visual recipe: a rectangular frame, centered headline, centered icon, and stacked copy. Many club posters use a similar centered burst with a pill-shaped center; many bar posters use a centered music symbol; many store posters use the same sale-burst treatment. That consistency makes the atlas feel mechanically generated even when the words and colors change.

Keep the visual language. Change the composition, focal image, and secondary shapes so each poster has its own reason to look the way it does.

## Recommended changes, in order

### 1. Vary the composition

Build a small library of layouts and choose the one that best fits each poster:

- Full-height illustration with a small title at the bottom.
- Large title crossing or partially covering the image.
- Off-center focal image balanced by a title or copy block on the opposite side.
- Diagonal image or lettering for motion.
- Split composition with a large image panel and a narrow information strip.
- Dense photocopied collage for a handbill or alley notice.

Do not make every design asymmetrical. Use centered layouts when they suit the poster; make the rest visibly different.

### 2. Give each poster a specific focal image

Replace some repeated generic icons and starbursts with images tied to the individual poster:

- A performer, microphone, instrument, or band-specific mark.
- A distinctive product or mascot for a store promotion.
- A character, object, or visual gag for a club or event.
- A handwritten symbol, tear-off tab, or layered notice for an alley handbill.

Draw or edit the important silhouette by hand. Procedural tools can repeat supporting stars, dots, borders, or print effects, but the focal image should feel chosen for that poster.

### 3. Give the viewer an eye path

Use a pose, cable, light beam, diagonal, or repeated shape to connect the focal image to the title and then to the date or offer. For example, a guitar cable can curl toward the venue name; a spotlight can point to the performer and event title; a product illustration can lead into a price block.

Keep these paths out of faces and important lettering. The goal is to make the parts feel connected, not to decorate every gap.

### 4. Add controlled hand-made variation

Try small, deliberate changes such as:

- A slightly uneven baseline or hand-adjusted letter.
- One broken or imperfect border.
- A two-pixel print-registration shift on one ink color.
- A few unique contour pixels on the main icon.
- Uneven spacing or a hand-drawn correction on a photocopied flyer.

Keep the pixel grid consistent. Avoid blur, antialiasing, and random single-pixel noise. Imperfection should look like a person made or printed the poster, not like an effect was applied everywhere.

### 5. Make each location feel materially different

| Location | Art direction | Useful surface cues |
|---|---|---|
| Club | Theatrical, flashy, or bargain-bin glam; use a performer, spotlight, marquee, or show-specific image | Glossy accent, faux foil, saturated ink, worn edges |
| Bar | Local and improvised; give each band or event its own symbol and lettering rhythm | Photocopy grain, tape, marker edits, date stamp, beer ring |
| Alley | Unofficial and layered; mix notices, handbills, corrections, and overlapping papers | Torn edges, paste stains, staple holes, skew, rain fade |
| Store | Lead with the product or offer; vary product image, price, and slogan hierarchy | Window-faded color, printed price sticker, slightly misregistered ink |

Do not apply every material cue to every poster. Choose one or two cues that explain where it came from and how it was handled.

## Pixel-art guardrails

- Design at the intended pixel resolution or at a consistent integer scale.
- Use clusters of pixels to describe a shape, light plane, shadow, or material.
- Make the silhouette and value separation work before adding texture.
- Use a few clear light and dark groups instead of shading every pixel.
- Check the actual in-game size. Small supporting copy can become texture; preserve the headline and main symbol.
- Avoid overly fine patterns that shimmer or disappear when the poster is viewed at a distance.

## Quick artist exercise

Choose one poster from the sheet. Keep its copy and palette, then make three art-directed versions:

1. Replace the centered icon with a unique, hand-edited focal silhouette.
2. Try three different compositions: centered, diagonal, and off-center.
3. Use one clear light or shape path to lead from the image to the headline.
4. Add one restrained printing or wear cue that fits the location.
5. View each version as a small thumbnail and in the game scene. Select the version that reads fastest and feels most specific.

Use that result to decide which layout and variation controls belong in the generator. Do not add random variation until the hand-directed version works.

## Review questions

- Can I tell what makes this poster different from the others before reading all the copy?
- Is there one clear focal image or symbol?
- Does the composition lead my eye somewhere?
- Are repeated motifs supporting the idea, or just filling empty space?
- Does the wear fit this poster's location and history?
- Does it still read at the actual in-game size?
